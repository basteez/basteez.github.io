Somewhere in the last six months, I stopped opening my editor on a Sunday.

Not the work one. The other one, where the projects nobody asked for live.

There was no burnout and no crisis, nothing you could point at, which is exactly why it took me so long to notice. Work was good, and busy in the way good work is. Reviews went fine, features landed, and every metric that measures a software engineer from the outside pointed up and to the right. So this isn't a story about a job, or a team, or a workload. On that front I've been lucky and I still am.

Nothing was wrong. Something was just gone.

## The symptom I ignored

Orgsidian sat untouched for months.

By itself that means nothing. Side projects die all the time; dying is most of what side projects do. What was different is that I never decided to stop. There was no evening where I sat there and thought "this isn't going anywhere". I just didn't open it. For a couple of weeks, then for a lot longer than that.

And when I finally did open it, the first thing I felt wasn't curiosity about where I'd left off. It was the urge to catch up. To drag the thing back to a state where it was worth having. Not to build anything. To close a gap.

I told myself I was tired. Maybe it was just a full stretch of life, more responsibilities than I used to have and less spare attention to spread around. Reasonable explanations were available and I took them, because they were true enough.

## Shipping more, enjoying it less

I want to be careful here, because the lazy version of this story is a complaint about AI making code worse, and **that isn't the story I have**.

The code was fine. It worked, most of it is still working, and if you'd asked me that year whether the tooling was worth it I'd have said obviously yes. I'd have been right, too.

What I remember about writing it is nothing.

There's no afternoon attached to it. No wrong turn I took first, no moment where it clicked, no detail I could tell you without going to look it up. It exists, my name is on the commits, and it left no trace in me at all.

That kept happening, and not only on the clock. It happened just as much on the things I was building for myself, which is the part that should have bothered me a lot sooner than it did.

## The pleasure was in the session, not the artifact

Here's what I think I had wrong: I assumed I liked building things. Objects. Repos, features, deployed URLs.

I don't think that was ever it. What I liked was the two hours.

You know the ones. You've got a rough idea and no plan. The obvious approach turns out to be wrong in some interesting way. You end up reading the source of a dependency because the docs won't tell you what you actually need to know. Then you look up and it's dark outside and you have maybe twenty lines to show for the whole evening, and you feel great.

AI is very good at compressing that, and it's supposed to be. Optimising for the artifact is the entire product. But when the artifact shows up without the two hours attached, you get everything you said you wanted and none of the thing you were there for.

I wrote about the other half of this a few months ago, in [AI and the Craft](/p/ai-and-the-craft-are-we-extending-ourselves-or-outsourcing-ourselves/): what you trade away when you let the tool do the thinking. That half is easier to talk about. "I understand my own systems less" sounds serious. "I'm not having fun" sounds like whining.

It isn't whining, though. It's the reason I have this career at all. I didn't arrive here through a plan, I arrived because at 15 I found this more interesting than anything else on offer. That kid is why I'm any good at the job now, and nobody was paying him. Whatever keeps him around seems worth maintaining on purpose, the same way you'd maintain anything else you depend on.

## So I'm starting a project with the AI switched off

Not as a protest. As an experiment, with one question behind it: is the thing I lost gone, or just crowded out?

The rules are deliberately dumb, because clever rules turn into a system you maintain instead of a project you build.

- No chat, no agents. I'm not disabling autocompletion (it's genuinely useful, and pretending I'd give it up would turn this into a performance). What stops is opening the chat panel to do my thinking for me.
- Docs are fine. Reading the source of my dependencies is better than fine, it's encouraged. Searching and forums are fine too.
- No deadline, no roadmap, no launch. If it never ships, the experiment still ran.
- One hour at a time, usually at the weekend, and **the session is the unit**. Not the milestone.

That last one is the whole point. If the session is the unit, an hour spent reading with nothing committed at the end still counts. Under the old accounting that hour was wasted.

## What I expect to go wrong

I haven't started yet. This is the last thing I'm writing before I do, so everything below is a guess, and guesses about your own behaviour are worth roughly what you'd expect.

The one I'm most sure of is the reaching. I know that reflex is there because I caught it earlier this year with autocomplete: opening a file and waiting for the suggestion before I'd finished deciding what I wanted. So I don't think the first thing I notice will be the slowness. I think it'll be my hand going for the shortcut, the way you reach for your phone in a lift. And not on the hard parts either, on the small annoying ones. The reflex has very little to do with difficulty and a lot to do with not wanting to sit in a pause.

Then the slowness, which will be real, and which I'm not going to dress up. Some of it is just dull. Reading a changelog to find one flag isn't craft, it's admin, and doing it by hand doesn't make it noble.

And the likeliest outcome of all: I do two or three sessions and quit. Not dramatically. The same way Orgsidian ended, by not being opened. If that's what happens I'll write that one down too, which is an easier promise to make now, while I still think it's unlikely.

## What I'm not claiming

This isn't an argument. It's one person and one project that hasn't started.

I haven't stopped using AI. I use it at work every day, on purpose, and I'd defend that. Refusing a tool that demonstrably works because using it feels bad is superstition, and shipping is still the job.

I'm not telling you to try this, either. There's a version of this post that ends in five bullet points about reclaiming your craft, and I'd rather not write it. I don't know that any of this generalises, and I don't know yet whether it'll work on me.

I'm also suspicious of the nostalgia in here. It's very easy to mix up "coding was more fun before AI" with "coding was more fun when I was younger and had fewer responsibilities". Some of what I've described is probably the second one wearing the costume of the first. I can't separate them cleanly, so I won't pretend I can.

What I'll say instead is narrower. What I lost wasn't skill and it wasn't time. It was the part of the work that happens while you're doing it, and that turns out to be the part I was in it for.

## The trap I can already see

One failure mode worth naming now, while it's still theoretical.

The risk is that the pleasure quietly relocates. That six months from now the good feeling comes from writing about the sessions instead of from the sessions. Same substitution I've just spent a whole post describing, one level up, except now the artifact is the post.

From the outside that looks identical to this going well. Output, consistency, a growing body of work. You'd only catch it from the inside, and probably late.

So, for the record: the sessions are the project. This is a note about them, written before the first one.
